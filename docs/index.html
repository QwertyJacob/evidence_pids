S1 Queues, starts and category priors

This section completes § 4.3 of the paper. It shows how the start of every queue is estimated (§ S1.1), where the starts and the category priors lie with respect to the threshold (§ S1.2), and why questions are not worth spending beyond the alarms (§ S1.3).

S1.1 How the starts are estimated

Every alarm of a tested queue starts at the share of attack windows in its source queue, smoothed by (10). The source queue is built by the same rule as the tested queue, but on the source collections. Table S1 gives the counts behind every start. It also gives the alarms of each tested queue, with their realised share of attack windows: the share that an exact start would match.

Validation days lower the starts. The source queues are built on both the validation and the test splits of the source collections. Validation days contain no annotated attack. They add alarms to the source queues, but no attack window, and so pull the starts down. Starts computed on the source test splits alone are 1.5 to 1.8 times higher. The alert queues are the exception. No validation window can score above the maximum of its own validation days, so validation days add no alert.

The smoothing matters most when a source queue holds no attack window. The start is then \(1/(n_N + 2)\), set by the smoothing alone, where the raw share of attack windows would be 0. Three starts of DARPA TC are of this kind: those of its queues of one and three alarms a day, and that of its alert queue. Two of them lie near the threshold. The queue of one alarm a day starts at exactly \(1/11\), from no attack window among 9 alarms: the threshold at \(r = 10\) (§ 3.2). The alert queue starts at \(1/9\), from no attack window among 7 alerts. Suppose half a window is added to each class instead of one, so that \(p_N = (a_N + 0.5)/(n_N + 1)\). These two starts then become 5.0 and 6.25 percent, both below the threshold. Table S4 gives every start under the three estimates, and § S4 reports how the results move with them.

S1.2 Where the starts and the category priors lie

Two kinds of start appear in this study. An alarm starts at the start of its queue. A window known only by its category \(k\) of the detector score (§ 4.1) starts at the prior of its category, \(p_k\) of (15). This prior is estimated like the start of a queue, but over all labelled source windows of the category rather than over the alarms of a queue. For example, in the model tested on OpTC host 051, 9 of the 120 labelled source alerts are attack windows. The prior of the alerts is then \(p_{\texttt{alert}} = 10/122 = 8.2\) percent, the same as the start of the alert queue (Table S1). Attack windows are far rarer among all windows of the other categories, so their priors are far smaller. A category with too few source windows takes the prior of another (§ A.2). Table S2 lists every prior.

The zone of a category. Each question has a zone (§ 3.5): the starting probabilities from which at least one of its answers changes the decision. The zone of a category is the union of the zones of its questions. A window that starts inside it can change its decision after one answer to some question. Most alarms are windows of category high. At \(r = 10\), where the threshold is 9.1 percent, the zone of high runs from 2.7 to 13.5 percent in the DARPA TC model, from 2.0 to 15.8 percent in the OpTC model and from 0.94 to 13.8 percent in the ATLASv2 model. Figure S1 places the zones, the starts of the queues and the category priors on one line.

Most category priors lie far below their zone. At its own prior, a window of category unscored or low starts between 0.06 and 0.41 percent. A window of category high starts between 0.36 and 0.78 percent. Every one of these starts lies below the zone of its category. By (5), evidence would have to multiply their odds by 12.6 to 162 to escalate them. For each of these categories, in every model, the strongest single answer falls short of the required factor by at least 2.2 times (Table S2).

Only the alerts of two models start inside their zone. The alerts of OpTC host 051 and ATLASv2 start at their priors of 8.2 and 8.1 percent, just under the threshold. Any answer that multiplies their odds by 1.12 or 1.13 escalates them.

Alarm queues start near the threshold. The starts of the queues lie between 1.26 and 11.32 percent (Table S1). The realistic queues hold 1, 3, 10 or 30 alarms a day, or the alerts. For these, the factor that evidence must supply lies between 0.78 and 3.3. A factor below 1 means that the queue starts above the threshold. An answer can then change the decision only by dividing the odds. For the queue of 100 alarms a day, the factor is 5.9 to 7.8.

Figures S2 and S3 repeat Figure S1 at the other two cost ratios of the realistic region.

S1.3 Questions beyond the alarms

The count of admissible questions (§ 5.1) can also be applied beyond the alarms. It then tells whether questions should be spent on ordinary windows at all. Suppose every one of the 133,397 test windows starts at the prior of its category. In any realistic cell, the models then admit a question on at most 7 windows, or 0.0052 percent. All 7 are alerts of OpTC host 051 and ATLASv2, the only windows whose priors lie inside their zone (Figure S1). This is the base rate effect of intrusion detection [20]. Most category priors lie far below the threshold, so no answer can lift them above it. At cost ratios of 50 and 100, outside the realistic region, the threshold is lower, and more windows come within reach.

S2 The fitted answer model

This section completes § 4.5 and § A.2 of the paper. It says which windows the tables count, lists the fitted likelihood ratios, and explains why the tables were not refitted on alarms.

S2.1 Windows without history

A question asks whether the files, addresses or processes of a window are new to the host, that is, first seen within the last 24 hours (§ 4.4). Its answer therefore needs 24 hours of history. Windows from the first 24 hours of the records of each collection have no such history. They are left out of two sets: the tested windows, and the counts from which the answer model estimates how often each answer occurs (§ 4.5).

The starts are different. They need no answer, only the Velox score and the label of each window, so the exclusion does not apply to them. For example, the validation split of ClearScope E3 lies entirely within its first 24 hours. None of its windows is tested or counted in the tables. Its alarms still count in the source queues when OpTC host 051 or ATLASv2 is tested (§ 4.3), all of them as benign windows.

S2.2 The fitted likelihood ratios

Table S2 gives the fitted model of each tested group. For each category of the detector score, it gives the prior of the category, the prior and tables that the category uses (§ A.2), and the fitted likelihood ratio of every single answer. A ratio above 1 raises the odds of attack, and a ratio below 1 lowers them. For example, in the DARPA TC model, a novel network answer on a window of category unscored multiplies the odds by 12, while on a window of category high it multiplies them by only 1.9.

S2.3 Why the tables were not refitted on alarms

The answers are weaker on alarms than over all windows of a category (§ A.3). A natural repair would refit the tables on the source alarm queues. The tables of each category were kept instead, for three reasons.

The attack half cannot be fitted on alarms. Each table has 27 cells, and the model gives a category its own tables only from 20 attack windows (§ A.2). The source queues of the realistic queues hold 0 to 16 attack windows each. In DARPA TC, the source queues of one and three alarms a day and of the alerts hold none. The source windows of category high, by contrast, hold 38 to 53 (Table A1). Only the benign half, where the difference lies, could be refitted on alarms. A ratio of a refitted half to a half that was not refitted, however, would hold for neither set of windows.

The tables explain only part of the miscalibration. In the queue of three a day, the questions realised more than the model expected. A start below the realised share produces this pattern, as in DARPA TC, where the start is 3.45 percent against a realised share of 20.8 percent. Weaker answers would produce the opposite pattern.

A refit adds no attack. The realistic queues would still hold 1 to 6 distinct attacks. Ranking the criteria needs hundreds to thousands (§ S5). New tables would be judged on the same few events.

S3 How realistic the example is

The realistic region of § 4.8 was fixed from published figures alone, before any outcome was computed. Its four axes rest on evidence of different strength. This section weighs each in turn.

Alarm volume. Only one published figure gives the alarms per monitored host. Dong et al. [47] report that practitioners accept 0.001 to 0.1 alarms per host per day. Every queue of this study exceeds this figure, by a factor of at least 5. The smallest excess is that of the queue of one alarm a day on ATLASv2, whose two hosts share a queue. The queues, however, are inputs to automated triage, not to analysts. Their sizes follow manual review capacity only as a workload reference. Nothing in the replay requires an analyst to review every incoming alarm. The escalations set the later workload of investigation, and the loss counts each benign escalation as wasted analyst effort. Staffing and queueing delays further downstream lie outside the replay. Measured volumes per SOC range widely: from hundreds of high priority alerts a day [24] to more than ten thousand alerts a day left to analysts after automatic blocking [48].

Minutes per alarm. The range of 10 to 40 minutes rests on three sources: one measurement in a user study [24], one estimate by experienced analysts [49], and one industry figure quoted by three papers [8, 58, 32]. It values the human effort wasted on a false escalation. Automated evidence actions are charged separately, through their price.

Cost ratio. No study measures the cost ratio. The region of 5 to 20 follows the only value used for alert triage, 10, tested from 5 to 20 [60]. The full sweep shows how the results move outside this region. At \(r = 50\) and \(r = 100\), the short queues lose every admissible question.

Prices. The realistic prices come from automated actions: LLM agents and sandboxes (Table 2). A question may instead stand for a full investigation by a second analyst. Its price is then near that of one false escalation, outside the region. At that price, no question is admissible on any queue.

S4 Sensitivity of the results

The results rest on choices that a careful analyst could make differently. This section changes them one at a time: the order in which the alarms of a day are visited (§ S4.1), the smoothing strength and the estimate of the starts (§ S4.2), and the weight of one collection among the alerts (§ S4.3).

S4.1 Order of visiting

The replay visits the alarms of a day from the top of the queue, in order of their Velox score (§ 4.6). Visiting them in order of their expected decision value instead changes the outcomes only in the queue of 100 alarms a day. There, it changes 420 of the 112,896 replayed combinations of collection, queue, cost ratio, price, budget and criterion.

S4.2 Smoothing strength and starts

Stronger smoothing. The study uses a smoothing strength of \(\tau = 10^4\) (§ 4.5). Stronger smoothing, \(\tau = 10^5\) or \(10^6\), pulls every likelihood ratio closer to 1. It leaves fewer admissible questions: 24 and 7 instead of 90 in the queue of ten a day. The losses then come close to those of asking nothing.

Weaker smoothing and other starts. Weaker smoothing, \(\tau = 10^2\) or \(10^3\), leaves most results in place, but not all. The same holds for the two other estimates of the starts of § S1.1: half a window added to each class instead of one, and source queues built on the test splits only. Table S3 repeats the value per question at \(r = 10\) and \(c = 0.01\) under each of these choices.

  • Half a window per class. The starts fall. The DARPA TC queue of one alarm a day and the DARPA TC alert queue then start below the threshold. The value in the queue of three a day falls from 1.3 to 0.4. The value in the queue of ten a day turns negative, to −0.14. The value among the alerts rises, to 0.28 under expected decision value.

  • Source test splits only. The starts rise. The values of the queues of three and ten a day become 0.84 and 0.46.

  • Weaker smoothing. Most values stay close. The queue of thirty a day, however, turns positive: 0.048, with 270 questions instead of 150. Information gain among the alerts rises from 0.016 to 0.58.

Over the realistic region, the sign of the value changes in 35 of 198 combinations with half a window per class, and in 46 of 174 with starts from the test splits only. It changes in 41 and 38 of 198 combinations with \(\tau = 10^2\) and \(10^3\). Most of these changes occur where the value lies close to zero.

Table S4 gives the start of every tested queue under the three estimates.

S4.3 The alert queue without ClearScope E3

One collection dominates the alert queue. ClearScope E3 contributes 102 of the 124 alerts, with 2 attack windows. Without it, the alert queue holds 22 labelled alerts and 7 attack windows. At \(r = 10\) and \(c = 0.01\), its decision loss is then 8 without questions, 9 under expected decision value, 8 under information gain, 7.5 under random choice and 8 with all questions: all policies lie within 1.5 of one another. With ClearScope E3, the differences are large: 107 under expected decision value, against 84.5 under random choice and 28 with all questions. These large differences therefore come from ClearScope E3.

The weight of ClearScope E3 has a likely cause. Of its test windows, 34 percent are high, against 3 to 19 percent in the other collections. Its validation split also lies within the first 24 hours of its records (§ S2.1). The maximum validation score, which defines an alert, may thus come from an unusually quiet period.

S5 How many attacks tell two criteria apart

Two criteria rarely take different decisions, and each different decision rests on one uncertain label. This section asks how many attacks a queue must hold before the labels can tell two criteria apart. It derives the numbers of Table S5 in three steps.

The difference on one alarm. Take two criteria \(\pi\) and \(\pi'\). For each alarm \(i\) of a queue, the recorded answers fix their decisions \(d_{\pi,i}\) and \(d_{\pi',i}\): 1 to escalate and 0 to dismiss. For random choice, the decision is the share of seeds that escalate. The label \(Y_i\) of the alarm is drawn from the model: it is 1 with probability \(p_i\), the model’s probability of attack after all the answers of the alarm. Holding the answers fixed understates the variability that new data would show. The loss of a decision \(d\) on the alarm is \(d + Y_i\,(r - (1+r)\,d)\). It is 1 for a false escalation (\(d = 1\), \(Y_i = 0\)), \(r\) for a missed attack window (\(d = 0\), \(Y_i = 1\)) and 0 otherwise. The difference in loss between the two criteria therefore has mean and variance

\[\begin{gathered} \mu_i = (d_{\pi,i} - d_{\pi',i})\,\bigl(1 - (1+r)\,p_i\bigr), \\ \sigma_i^2 = (1+r)^2\,(d_{\pi,i} - d_{\pi',i})^2\,p_i\,(1 - p_i). \end{gathered} \tag{S1}\]

Both are zero when the two criteria take the same decision on the alarm: only alarms with different decisions contribute.

The number of alarms. For an alarm drawn at random from the queue, the difference has mean \(\mu\), the average of the \(\mu_i\). Its variance \(\sigma^2\) is the average of the \(\sigma_i^2\) plus the variance of the \(\mu_i\). Let \(z_q\) denote the quantile at probability \(q\) of the standard normal distribution. A two sided test at the 5 percent level detects the difference with probability 0.8 after

\[n = \left(\frac{(z_{0.975} + z_{0.8})\,\sigma}{|\mu|}\right)^2 \approx \left(\frac{2.8\,\sigma}{|\mu|}\right)^2 \tag{S2}\]

alarms. The smaller the average difference relative to its spread, the more alarms are needed.

From alarms to attacks. Alarms convert into attack windows through the average of the \(p_i\). Attack windows convert into distinct attacks through the number of attack windows per annotated attack in the same queue. For example, suppose two criteria differ by \(\mu = 0.1\) per alarm, with \(\sigma = 1.5\). Then \(n = (2.8 \times 15)^2 \approx 1{,}770\) alarms. With an average \(p_i\) of 5 percent, these hold about 88 attack windows. At 10 attack windows per attack, they hold about 9 distinct attacks. A closed form of this kind was checked against direct simulation of the labels.

The result. Every realistic queue holds 1 to 6 distinct attacks (Table S5). Telling expected decision value apart from random choice, or from information gain, needs hundreds to thousands. Only the comparison with asking nothing needs fewer than a hundred.

It adds two analyses that the paper only mentions: the shared daily budget (§ S6.3) and the realised value of a question (§ S6.4). Unless stated otherwise, every count sums over the three tested groups.

S6.1 When a question is worth asking

Table 3 of the paper counts the alarms with an admissible question at \(r = 10\). Figure S4 extends this count to every cost ratio, as a share of each queue.

S6.2 Where the criteria can differ

Section 5.2 sorts the questioned alarms into blocks: alarms of one tested group and queue that share their tables and their available questions. Table S6 lists every block of the realistic queues at \(r = 10\) and \(c = 0.01\). In 20 of the 24 blocks, expected decision value and information gain ask the same question, so they take the same decision on every alarm. S6.3 How many questions a day

This section asks how many questions the daily budget should allow. It also asks what a larger budget changes, in attack windows caught and in false escalations.

A bound known before any question. With one question per alarm, a day can use at most as many questions as it has alarms with an admissible question. A larger budget changes nothing on that day. The fitted model counts these alarms without labels. At \(r = 10\) and \(c = 0.01\), there are at most 1 per collection and day in the queue of one a day, 3 in the queue of three a day and 10 in the queue of ten a day. In the queue of thirty a day, there are 30 on 5 of the 13 collection days and none on the others. Among the alerts, there are up to 84, with a median of 4 over the 7 collection days with alerts. A budget of 3 questions a day thus already covers the queue of three a day, and a budget of 10 covers the queue of ten a day.

What the questions achieve needs the labels. Figure S5 follows the queues of 3, 10 and 30 alarms a day and the alert queue, at \(r = 10\) and a price of 0.01. Table S8 gives every value. Two patterns stand out.

With a small budget, the criteria end close to one another. With one question a day, at most one alarm per collection and day is questioned. This gives 9 questioned alarms in the queues of three and ten a day, 5 in the queue of thirty a day and 7 among the alerts.

  • In the queue of three a day, the single daily question catches 1 of the 5 attack windows, all of which asking nothing misses. The decision loss falls from 65 to between 52 and 54, under every criterion.

  • In the queue of ten a day, the 9 questions catch 1 attack window, and the loss falls from 120 to about 111. This is a saving of about 1.0 per question, four times the value per question without a budget limit.

  • In the queue of thirty a day and among the alerts, the single daily question changes the loss by at most two units.

With a large budget, the queue and the price decide. Without a budget limit, at \(r = 10\) and \(c = 0.01\), the outcome differs from queue to queue. Figures S6 to S11 show the other cost ratios and prices.

  • In the queue of ten a day, expected decision value and information gain catch 5 of the 12 attack windows, at the cost of 27 false escalations. Random choice catches 4.6, with 28.5 false escalations. Asking nothing catches none and escalates nothing. The decision loss is 97 for both criteria based on the model and 102.5 for random choice, against 120 without questions.

  • In the queue of thirty a day, 150 questions catch 3 of the 19 attack windows, with 46 to 48 false escalations. The loss is 206 to 208, against 190 without questions.

  • Among the alerts, asking nothing already escalates all 9 attack windows. Expected decision value and information gain keep them all, and escalate 107 and 106 benign alerts, against 108 without questions. Random choice escalates only 73.5 benign alerts, but loses 1.1 attack windows.

How many questions an alarm may receive. The daily budget is one design choice. The number of questions an alarm may receive is another, and it matters more. The largest change in this study comes not from the criterion but from the rule of one question per alarm. Under a sequential rule, an alarm receives a further question as long as some unasked question is still admissible at its updated probability, with no budget limit. At \(r = 10\) and \(c = 0.01\), this rule changes the losses as follows.

  • In the alert queue, it catches 7 of the 9 attack windows, with only 8 false escalations. The decision loss falls to 28, the loss of asking all three questions of every alarm. With one question chosen by expected decision value, the loss is 107, and without questions 108.

  • In the queue of ten a day, the same rule raises the loss from 97 to 128, above the 120 of asking nothing.

  • In the queue of three a day, it raises the loss from 29 to 60.

Under the sequential rule, every criterion reaches nearly the same loss, within 2 in every realistic queue. The choice of the first question thus matters even less. Further questions help where a later answer can overturn a misleading earlier one. They hurt where each further answer adds a chance of a false escalation. A rule that plans several questions ahead, rather than one at a time, is not studied here. Section S4 reports two further checks: the order in which alarms are visited, and the smoothing strength.

Table S8 gives every value at \(r = 10\) and \(c = 0.01\), for every queue and budget. Table S9 gives the decision loss of every criterion without budget limit, including all six fixed orders.

S6.4 What a question is worth

Once the labels are read, the outcomes give the value per question \(v\) of (13): how much decision loss a question saved. Like the price \(c\), it is measured in false escalations. Where \(v \ge 0\), it is the break even price of the questions asked. A designer can compare it directly with the price of an evidence fetcher (Table 2), or convert it into analyst minutes. The values of this section are those of the proxy questions of the worked example, whose model fails its own check (§ 5.3). They show how the measure reads, not what costly evidence would be worth (§ 5.5).

Figure S12 plots \(v\) against the price, at \(r = 10\). Each panel shows one criterion, and each line one alarm queue. A point above the dotted line means that, in that queue and at that price, the questions that the criterion asks save more loss in total than they cost.

Value on the alarm queues. At prices up to 0.01, a question saved, depending on the criterion:

  • 0.31 to 0.62 false escalations in the queue of one a day;

  • 1.3 to 1.4 in the queue of three a day;

  • 0.19 to 0.26 in the queue of ten a day;

  • 0.0081 to 0.19 among the alerts.

In the queue of thirty a day, a question saved about 0.05 at a price of 0.001, but already lost 0.11 at 0.01.

Value falls as the price rises. As the price rises, only the questions with the largest expected decision value stay admissible. On the larger queues, these questions increase the decision loss. In the queue of ten a day, the value falls to −0.14 at a price of 0.03, to −0.42 at 0.1 and to −0.71 at 0.3. Among the alerts, it falls to −0.86 under expected decision value from a price of 0.03.

Over the realistic region. A question was worth between a loss of 0.86 false escalations, at \(r = 10\), and a saving of 3.5, at \(r = 20\). In each queue, 12 to 14 combinations of cost ratio and price had admissible questions, and the net value was positive in 4 to 11 of them. The three criteria agreed on the sign of the net value in 65 of these 66 combinations. In this replay, whether a question saves more than it costs thus depends mainly on the queue and the price. The reason is that every criterion questions the same alarms, and the criteria choose different questions on few of them (§ 5.2).

Figures S13 and S14 repeat Figure S12 at the other two cost ratios of the realistic region, for expected decision value (EDV), information gain (IG) and random choice. They are read in the same way. In addition, a dashed line gives all test windows under the same filter, next to the solid lines of the alarm queues.

How certain these values are. Each value of \(v\) rests on few events, so another set of recorded days could have given a very different value. A bootstrap interval measures how different. The replay runs once, and its decision losses and questions are summed per collection day: 13 days for each daily queue, and 7 for the alert queue. A resampled data set draws as many days as were recorded, at random and with replacement, so that some days count twice and others not at all. The value \(v\) is then recomputed on the resampled set. Whole days are drawn, not single windows, because the windows of one attack fall on the same day. The central 95 percent of the values of \(v\), over 2,000 resampled data sets, form the interval. An interval entirely above zero means that the questions saved loss even in unfavourable resamples. An interval that contains zero means that the data cannot tell whether the questions saved loss or added it.

Two examples. The saving of 3.5 per question comes from the queue of three a day, at \(r = 20\) and \(c = 0.001\). It comes entirely from two collection days of DARPA TC: one day of CADETS E3 and one of THEIA E3, both on 12 April 2018. On these days, 6 of the 28 questions catch the 5 attack windows of the queue, which belong to 2 attacks. Resampled data sets without these two days give a value near zero, and those that draw them several times give up to 8.6. The interval runs from −0.071 to 8.6, so the saving may well be zero. The loss of 0.86 per question comes from the alerts, at \(r = 10\) and \(c = 0.03\). It rests on 7 questions, all on benign alerts, 6 of which end in a false escalation. Its interval, from −1 to −0.67, lies entirely below zero.

Most intervals contain zero. At \(r = 10\) and \(c = 0.01\), the intervals lie above zero in the queues of one and three alarms a day. For example, the interval of expected decision value in the queue of one a day runs from 0.077 to 0.54. In the other queues, the intervals contain zero. Over the 198 combinations of realistic queue, cost ratio, price and criterion with questions, 161 intervals contain zero, 24 lie entirely above it and 13 entirely below it. In most combinations, the data cannot tell whether a question saved loss or added it. Table S10 gives the values of Figure S12 at prices of 0.01 and 0.03, with their bootstrap intervals and the model intervals of § 5.3.

Where the value comes from. A question saves decision loss by catching attack windows, and adds loss through false escalations. Take the queue of ten a day at \(r = 10\) and \(c = 0.01\). There, the 90 questions of expected decision value catch 5 attack windows and add 27 false escalations. The catches are worth \(5 \times 10 = 50\), and the false escalations cost 27. The net saving is 23 over 90 questions, or 0.26 each. At 20 minutes per false escalation, the 27 false escalations cost 540 minutes of analyst attention, or 6 minutes per question. The saving thus lies in attack windows caught, not in analyst time.

Attacks counted whole. An attack is caught when at least one of its windows is escalated. In the queues of three, ten and thirty a day, asking nothing catches no attack. With questions, 1 of the 2 attacks is caught in the queue of three a day, 2 of the 3 in the queue of ten a day, and 2 of the 6 in the queue of thirty a day. Asking nothing already catches the only attack of the queue of one a day, and all 3 attacks among the alerts (Table S11).

In minutes. At 10 to 40 minutes per false escalation, the realistic values range from 8.6 to 34 minutes lost per question to 35 to 140 minutes saved. These are minutes of loss. As the example above shows, they mix analyst time with missed attack windows weighted by \(r\). For comparison, at 20 minutes per false escalation, a question at a price of 0.01 costs the equivalent of 12 seconds of analyst attention.

How robust the values are. Two other reasonable estimates of the starts, and weaker smoothing, change the sign of the value in 35 of 198 to 46 of 174 realistic combinations with questions (§ S4.2, Table S3). Most of these changes occur where the value lies close to zero. The values of this section are therefore not robust to choices that a careful analyst could make differently.

Starts below the realised share. In the DARPA TC queues of three and ten alarms a day, the start is far lower than the share of attack windows actually found in the queue: 3.45 against 20.8 percent, and 3.26 against 12.5 percent (Table S1). At \(r = 10\), the start lies below the threshold of 9.1 percent, and the realised share above it. With the starts used, every alarm of these queues is dismissed unless an answer raises it above the threshold. With starts equal to the realised shares, every alarm would be escalated with no question at all. In these queues, the questions thus mostly lift alarms over a threshold that a correct start would already exceed. Part of the value measured there, including the largest of this section, therefore comes from correcting a start that is too low. It does not come from information about each alarm.

Most alerts come from ClearScope E3. ClearScope E3 contributes 102 of the 124 alerts. The large differences among the alerts come from it, such as 107 under expected decision value against 84.5 under random choice. Without it, every policy reaches a decision loss between 7.5 and 9 (§ S4.3).

Fixed orders. In two queues, some fixed orders do much better than the criteria based on the model (Table S9, at \(r = 10\) and \(c = 0.01\)). Among the alerts, the two orders that ask the network question first reach a loss of 27, against 107 for expected decision value and 108 without questions. In the queue of one a day, the orders that start with the network or the process question reach 2 to 4, against 8. Under a correct model, expected decision value chooses the question worth most, and another choice should rarely end with a lower loss. These differences rest on few events and are not a ranking. They are, however, a further sign that the model is miscalibrated (§ 5.3).

All test windows. Without the filter, information gain and random choice ask 133,307 questions at \(r = 10\), one on every labelled test window. They raise the decision loss from 3,010 to about 3,015, or about \(-0.00004\) false escalations per question. Expected decision value asks only where some answer can change the decision. Even without the filter, it therefore asks only the 7 alerts that the filter keeps (§ S1.3). With the filter, these 7 alerts are the only questions on all test windows. They lose 0.86 per question under expected decision value, 0.71 under information gain and 0.66 under random choice. This is little in total, but the worst value per question of this study.

S7 Implementation and checks of the replay

This section gives the rules that settle ties and rounding, which a reimplementation needs (§ S7.1), and the checks that the replay passed (§ S7.2).

S7.1 Ties and numerical tolerance

  • Ties in a daily queue. Windows with the same Velox score are ordered by process identifier, and then by start time.

  • Ties between questions. When a criterion scores two admissible questions equally, it asks the first in the order file, network, process.

  • Numerical tolerance. At \(c = 0\), a question is admissible when \(\mathrm{EDV} > 10^{-12}\), so that rounding errors do not admit a question whose expected decision value is zero.

S7.2 Checks of the replay

The replay passed three checks.

  • An independent implementation. A second implementation of the same selection rules was written. Without filter, without shared budget and with one question per alarm, it reproduces every loss of the replay to within \(10^{-12}\).

  • The filter and the shared budget. The budget assigns questions down the queue. A replay with a budget of \(B\) questions should therefore match the replay without limit, cut after the first \(B\) questioned alarms of each day. Every replay with a finite budget was run explicitly and compared with this cut, alarm by alarm. Of 10,059,840 comparisons, none differed.

  • The summary results. Every one of the 470,400 values of the summary results was rebuilt exactly from the records of the individual alarms.
